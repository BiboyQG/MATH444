\section*{Exercise 1}
Let $(b_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a bounded sequence, and $(a_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence with 
\[\lim a_n = 0.\]

Show that 
\[\lim(a_n b_n) = 0.\]

\textbf{Solution:} \\

Since $(b_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a bounded sequence, there exists $M > 0$ such that
\[|b_n| \leq M \quad \text{for all } n \in \mathbb{N}\]

Since $\lim a_n = 0$, for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
\[|a_n| < \frac{\epsilon}{M} \quad \text{for all } n \geq N\]

Based on the above, for all $n \geq N$, we have:
\[|a_n b_n| = |a_n| \cdot |b_n| \leq |a_n| \cdot M < \frac{\epsilon}{M} \cdot M = \epsilon\]

Therefore, for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
\[|a_n b_n| < \epsilon \quad \text{for all } n \geq N\]

By the definition of the limit, we have
\[\lim(a_n b_n) = 0\]

\section*{Exercise 2}
Compute $\lim \frac{n!}{n^n}$, or show that this limit does not exist. \\

\textbf{Solution:} \\

Define $a_n = \frac{n!}{n^n}$ and find the ratio of consecutive terms:

\begin{align*}
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} &= \frac{(n+1)!/(n+1)^{n+1}}{n!/n^n} \\
&= \frac{(n+1) \cdot n! \cdot n^n}{n! \cdot (n+1)^{n+1}} \\
&= \frac{(n+1) \cdot n^n}{(n+1) \cdot (n+1)^n} \\
&= \frac{n^n}{(n+1)^n} \\
&= \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n < 1 \quad \text{(since $\frac{n}{n+1} < 1$)}
\end{align*}

Since all the terms are nonzero, by Ratio Test for convergence, we have:
\[
\lim \frac{n!}{n^n} = 0
\]

\section*{Exercise 3}
Let $(x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a sequence of positive real numbers such that $\lim x_n^{1/n} = L$ with $L < 1$. Prove that $\lim x_n = 0$. \\

\textbf{Solution:} \\

Let $r \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $L < r < 1$. \\

By the definition of limit, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n \geq N$:
\begin{align*}
|x_n^{1/n} - L| < r - L
\end{align*}

So we have:
\begin{align*}
x_n^{1/n} &< L + (r - L)\\
&= r
\end{align*}

Therefore, for all $n \geq N$, we have:
\begin{align*}
x_n &< r^n
\end{align*}

Since $0 < x_n < r^n$ for all $n \geq N$ and $\lim r^n = 0$ (since $r < 1$), by the Squeeze Theorem, we conclude that:
\begin{align*}
\lim x_n = 0
\end{align*}

\section*{Exercise 4}
Define the sequence $(x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ by $x_1 = 1$ and $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}$. Show that $(x_n)$ converges and find the limit. \\

\textbf{Solution:} \\

$\text{For } n = 1: x_2 = \sqrt{2 + x_1} = \sqrt{3} > 1 = x_1$ \\

$\text{For } n \geq 2, \text{ we want to show that the sequence is monotonically increasing by showing }$
\begin{align*}
    x_{n+1} > x_n \\ 
    \Leftrightarrow \sqrt{2 + x_n} > x_n
\end{align*}

$\text{Squaring both sides (since both sides are positive):}$
\begin{align*}
2 + x_n &> x_n^2 \\
\Leftrightarrow 2 &> x_n^2 - x_n \\
\Leftrightarrow 0 &> x_n^2 - x_n - 2 \\
\Leftrightarrow 0 &> (x_n - 2)(x_n + 1)
\end{align*}

Solving the inequality, we get $x_n < 2$. So we will prove that the sequence is bounded above by 2 using induction.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\text{Base case:}$ for $n = 1, x_1 = 1 < 2.$
    \item $\text{Inductive step:}$ assume $x_k < 2$ for some $k \in \mathbb{N}.$
    \item $\text{Then } x_{k+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_k} < \sqrt{2 + 2} = 2.$
\end{itemize}

$\text{By mathematical induction, } x_n < 2 \text{ for all } n \in \mathbb{N}.$ And since $x_1 = 1$, and $x_{n+1} = \sqrt{2 + x_n}$, we have $x_n > 1$. Therefore, $1 < x_n < 2$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ \\

By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, since $(x_n)$ is increasing and bounded above, it converges to some limit $L$ with $L \leq 2.$ \\

$\text{Using the recurrence relation and taking limits on both sides:}$
\begin{align*}
    L &= \sqrt{2 + L}
\end{align*}

$\text{So, } L = 2 \text{ or } L = -1. \text{ Since the sequence is positive, the limit } L = 2.$ \\

$\text{Therefore, the sequence } (x_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}} \text{ converges to } 2.$
